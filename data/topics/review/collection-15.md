## Judicial Power and Justiciability

### Judicial review

- **Marbury v. Madison** (1803) [high]. Also: Congressional control of federal jurisdiction, Remedies and scope of relief, Appointments and removal
  The Court held that Section 13 of the Judiciary Act, by adding to the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III, was void, and grounded the courts' power to refuse enforcement of unconstitutional statutes in the written Constitution, the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause.

### Congressional control of federal jurisdiction

- **Ex Parte McCardle** (1869) [high]. Also: Legislative interference with judicial decisions
  The Court dismissed McCardle's pending habeas appeal because the 1868 act repealing its appellate jurisdiction was a valid exception under Article III's Exceptions and Regulations Clause, and it declined to inquire into Congress's motives.

### Legislative interference with judicial decisions

- **United States v. Klein** (1872) [high]. Also: Congressional control of federal jurisdiction, Pardon power
  The Court held the 1870 proviso unconstitutional because it prescribed a rule of decision in pending cases, crossing from legislative into judicial power, even though framed as a withdrawal of jurisdiction, and also because it impaired the effect of the President's pardon.
- **Bank Markazi v. Peterson** (2016) [high]. Also: Treaty and foreign affairs power
  The Court held that § 8772, though it named a pending case by docket number, did not usurp the judicial power under Klein because it supplied new substantive standards for the District Court to apply rather than directing a result under old law.
- **Robertson v. Seattle Audubon Society** (1992) [high]
  The Court held that subsection (b)(6)(A) of the Northwest Timber Compromise validly amended the applicable environmental law rather than directing results in the pending spotted-owl cases, so it did not violate the Klein rule against legislative interference with judicial decisions.
- **Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc.** (1995) [high]. Also: Advisory opinions
  The Court held § 27A(b) unconstitutional because Article III's separation of legislative and judicial power bars Congress from retroactively requiring federal courts to reopen final judgments; the course note also uses the case as a type of forbidden advisory opinion.
- **United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians** (1980) [medium]. Also: Takings, Political question doctrine
  The course note assigns the case as a contrast with Plaut on legislative interference with judgments, and the Court held that Congress's mere waiver of the res judicata effect of a prior judgment favoring the United States does not violate separation of powers, distinguishing Klein; it also held that the 1877 Act effected a Fifth Amendment taking requiring just compensation.

### Standing: injury in fact

- **City of Los Angeles v. Lyons** (1983) [high]. Also: Remedies and scope of relief, Mootness
  The Court held Lyons lacked standing to seek an injunction because his past chokehold did not show a real and immediate threat of future injury, though he could pursue damages; it also held equitable relief unavailable absent irreparable injury and rejected the capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review exception.
- **Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife** (1992) [high]. Also: Standing: causation and redressability, Standing: congressionally created rights and injuries, Standing: generalized grievances
  The Court held the members' vague 'some day' plans to revisit foreign habitats were not an actual or imminent injury, doubted redressability because non-party agencies funded the projects, and rejected the idea that the ESA citizen-suit provision could convert a generalized grievance into an Article III injury.
- **Clapper v. Amnesty International USA** (2013) [high]. Also: Standing: causation and redressability
  The Court held respondents lacked standing because their feared interception under § 1881a was too speculative to be 'certainly impending,' that they could not manufacture standing through self-inflicted countermeasure costs, and alternatively that any interception was not fairly traceable to § 1881a given the Government's other surveillance methods.
- **Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins** (2016) [high]. Also: Standing: congressionally created rights and injuries
  The Court vacated because the Ninth Circuit analyzed only particularization and not the independent concreteness requirement of injury in fact, holding that a bare statutory (FCRA) violation divorced from concrete harm does not satisfy Article III even though Congress may elevate intangible harms.
- **Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus** (2014) [medium]. Also: Ripeness
  Although the course note assigns the case for ripeness, the Court held that petitioners' credible threat of enforcement (a prior probable-cause finding, the fact that anyone may file complaints, and the State's refusal to disavow enforcement) was an Article III injury in fact, and it treated the prudential ripeness factors as easily satisfied.
- **First Choice Women's Resource Centers, Inc. v. Davenport** (2026) [low]. Also: Mootness, Ripeness, Expressive association
  According to the course note's summary (no Tortwell brief exists), the Court held that New Jersey's donor-information subpoena could inflict present Article III injury by chilling association before any enforcement, and that narrowing the subpoena after suit did not moot the case; although the case sits in a mootness module, the central holding as summarized concerns injury in fact.

### Standing: causation and redressability

- **Allen v. Wright** (1984) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact, Standing: generalized grievances
  The Court held the parents lacked standing because the stigma and 'right to lawful government conduct' theories were not judicially cognizable injuries, and the cognizable injury (diminished integrated education) was not fairly traceable to the IRS's tax exemptions given the speculative chain of independent third-party decisions.
- **Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski** (2021) [high]. Also: Mootness, Remedies and scope of relief
  The Court held that a request for nominal damages satisfies Article III redressability for a completed violation of a legal right, which kept the claim live after the injunctive claim became moot; this follows the holding rather than the module's 'generalized grievances and taxpayer standing' heading, a doctrine the opinion does not address.
- **Warth v. Seldin** (1975) [high]. Also: Standing: third-party standing, Standing: associational and organizational standing, Standing: taxpayer standing
  The Court held the low-income plaintiffs lacked standing because their inability to obtain Penfield housing stemmed from housing-market economics and developers' independent choices, so there was no substantial probability that striking the zoning would redress their injury; it also rejected the taxpayers' and associations' claims on third-party-rights and associational grounds.
- **Duke Power Co. v. Carolina Environmental Study Group, Inc.** (1978) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact, Economic substantive due process, Ripeness
  The Court found standing because the District Court's finding that the McGuire and Catawba plants likely would not be built or operated without the Price-Anderson liability cap supplied the causal link to appellees' environmental injuries (thermal pollution and radiation), and it then upheld the cap as rational economic regulation under the Fifth Amendment's due process and equal protection components.
- **Linda R. S. v. Richard D.** (1973) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact
  The Court held the mother lacked standing because prosecuting the father under Article 602 would likely result only in his jailing, making any resulting support payments speculative, so there was no direct nexus between her injury and the nonenforcement she challenged.
- **Diamond Alternative Energy, LLC v. Environmental Protection Agency** (2025) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact
  The Court held the fuel producers had Article III standing because commonsense economic inferences and record evidence showed that invalidating EPA's approval of California's regulations would likely cause automakers to sell more gasoline vehicles, redressing the producers' injury, even though the producers were not the direct objects of the regulation.
- **Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization** (1976) [high]. Also: Standing: associational and organizational standing
  The Court held the indigent plaintiffs lacked standing because their denials of hospital care were not fairly traceable to the IRS ruling and it was speculative that reversing the ruling would lead independent third-party hospitals, not before the court, to serve them.
- **California v. Texas** (2021) [high]. Also: Standing: states and government plaintiffs, Advisory opinions
  The Court held that neither the individual nor the state plaintiffs had standing because, with the penalty zeroed out, no injury was fairly traceable to enforcement of the unenforceable §5000A(a), and a declaratory judgment alone would amount to an advisory opinion.

### Standing: taxpayer standing

- **Flast v. Cohen** (1968) [high]. Also: Standing: generalized grievances, Establishment Clause
  The Court held that federal taxpayers have Article III standing under a two-part nexus test (a challenge to an exercise of the taxing and spending power, plus a claim that it violates a specific limitation on that power such as the Establishment Clause), carving an exception to Frothingham.
- **Hein v. Freedom From Religion Foundation, Inc.** (2007) [high]. Also: Standing: generalized grievances, Establishment Clause
  The plurality held that federal taxpayers lack standing under the Flast v. Cohen exception to challenge, on Establishment Clause grounds, discretionary Executive Branch spending from general appropriations, because that spending was not expressly authorized or mandated by Congress and so lacks the required logical nexus to congressional taxing and spending power.
- **Arizona Christian School Tuition Organization v. Winn** (2011) [low]. Also: Standing: generalized grievances, Establishment Clause
  Per the course note, the Court held that Arizona's tuition tax credits are not government expenditures for purposes of the Flast v. Cohen exception, so the taxpayers challenging them under the Establishment Clause lacked standing.

### Standing: third-party standing

- **Singleton v. Wulff** (1976) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact
  The Court held that physicians denied Medicaid reimbursement had Article III injury and could assert their patients' abortion rights, based on the close doctor-patient relationship and genuine obstacles to the women suing themselves; the brief shows both factors were found present, so it does not support the course note's claim that only one is needed.
- **Craig v. Boren** (1976) [high]. Also: Sex classifications, Mootness
  The course note assigns Craig in a third-party standing module, and the brief confirms the Court held that the beer vendor Whitener could assert her male customers' equal protection rights because the statute operated directly on vendors; the Court also announced intermediate scrutiny for gender classifications.
- **Elk Grove Unified School District v. Newdow** (2004) [high]
  The Court held Newdow lacked prudential standing because his claim derived from his relationship with his daughter, state law denied him next-friend status, and his interests potentially conflicted with hers, so it never reached the Establishment Clause merits.
- **Barrows v. Jackson** (1953) [high]. Also: State action, Race classifications, Contracts Clause
  The course note assigns the case for third-party standing, and the Court let a white covenantor sued for damages assert the equal protection rights of absent non-Caucasian buyers who could not effectively present their own grievance, while also holding that damages enforcement of a racial covenant is state action and rejecting the Contract Clause claim because that clause reaches only legislation.
- **Gilmore v. Utah** (1976) [high]
  The Court terminated the stay sought by Gilmore's mother because Gilmore had competently made a knowing and intelligent waiver of his federal rights, leaving no basis for a third party to litigate on his behalf. The dissents' argument that he could not waive the Eighth Amendment question frames the next-friend standing problem.
- **June Medical Services L. L. C. v. Russo** (2020) [high]. Also: Reproductive rights
  The course note assigns June Medical for third-party standing, and the Court held that the third-party standing limit is prudential, that Louisiana waived its objection by conceding standing, and that providers may assert their patients' rights; it also struck down Act 620 as an undue burden on abortion access.

### Standing: states and government plaintiffs

- **Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency** (2007) [high]. Also: Standing: injury in fact, Standing: causation and redressability, Standing: congressionally created rights and injuries
  The Court held that Massachusetts, as a sovereign entitled to 'special solicitude' and holding a procedural right under the Clean Air Act, satisfied injury (loss of coastal land), causation (U.S. vehicle emissions' meaningful contribution), and redressability (incremental reduction suffices).

### Standing: congressionally created rights and injuries

- **TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez** (2021) [low]. Also: Standing: injury in fact, Standing: generalized grievances, Standing: taxpayer standing
  Per the course note, the Court held that only class members whose misleading credit reports were disclosed to third parties suffered a concrete harm analogous to defamation, so Congress's creation of a statutory cause of action under the FCRA could not by itself confer standing on the rest of the class.

### Ripeness

- **Poe v. Ullman** (1961) [high]. Also: Identifying fundamental rights, Family, marriage, and parental rights
  The plurality dismissed the appeals because Connecticut's decades-long nonenforcement of its contraception ban meant there was no real threat of prosecution and hence no controversy of sufficient immediacy, while Harlan's dissent substantially addressed the merits of marital privacy under Fourteenth Amendment due process.
- **Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner** (1967) [high]
  The Court held the drug makers' pre-enforcement challenge to the FDA labeling rule ripe under a two-part test—fitness of a purely legal issue for decision and hardship from forcing a choice between costly compliance and serious penalties.
- **United Public Workers of America v. Mitchell** (1947) [high]. Also: Advisory opinions, Public employee and student speech
  The Court held that the employees who merely wished to engage in political activity, absent a completed violation or definite threat of enforcement, presented no justiciable controversy and sought an advisory opinion, while Poole's claim, based on an actual charge for a completed act, was justiciable and his Hatch Act challenge failed on the merits.
- **Regional Rail Reorganization Act Cases** (1974) [medium]. Also: Takings, What process is due
  The course note assigns the case for ripeness, and the Court held the conveyance-taking claims ripe because implementation would lead inexorably to an irreversible conveyance, while valuation questions were premature; most of the opinion, though, resolves whether a Tucker Act remedy provides adequate just compensation for the alleged takings.
- **Lake Carriers' Assn. v. MacMullan** (1972) [high]. Also: Abstention
  The Court held the challenge ripe because Michigan's insistence that vessel owners promptly install sewage storage devices coerced present, costly compliance despite delayed prosecution, but it affirmed Pullman abstention so state courts could construe the ambiguous statute.

### Mootness

- **Moore v. Ogilvie** (1969) [high]. Also: Voting rights, Fundamental rights under equal protection, Redistricting and gerrymandering
  The course note assigns the case for mootness, and the brief confirms the Court held the post-election challenge not moot because the signature-distribution burden is 'capable of repetition, yet evading review'; on the merits it held that the rule violates equal protection under one-person, one-vote principles and overruled MacDougall v. Green.
- **Roe v. Wade** (1973) [medium]. Also: Reproductive rights, Identifying fundamental rights
  The course note assigns Roe for its holding that a challenge to an abortion ban is not mooted by the end of the plaintiff's pregnancy, because pregnancy is 'capable of repetition, yet evading review.' The brief omits that threshold ruling and covers only the merits holding that Fourteenth Amendment privacy encompasses the abortion decision, which is tagged as secondary.
- **DeFunis v. Odegaard** (1974) [high]
  The Court held the case moot because DeFunis, in his final quarter, would graduate regardless of the merits, and it rejected both the voluntary-cessation and capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review exceptions, so it never reached the equal protection challenge to race-conscious admissions.
- **Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.** (2000) [high]. Also: Standing: causation and redressability, Standing: injury in fact
  The Court held that Laidlaw's post-suit compliance did not moot FOE's civil-penalty claim because a defendant claiming voluntary cessation bears the heavy burden of showing the violations could not reasonably be expected to recur. It also held that civil penalties redress citizen plaintiffs by deterring ongoing and future violations, and that members' curtailed recreational use of the river was injury in fact.
- **United States Parole Commission v. Geraghty** (1980) [high]
  The Court held that a class action does not become moot when the named plaintiff's substantive claim expires after certification was denied, because he retains a personal stake in appealing the denial of class certification.

### Political question doctrine

- **Baker v. Carr** (1962) [high]. Also: Redistricting and gerrymandering, Standing: injury in fact, Voting rights
  The Court held that an equal protection challenge to Tennessee's legislative malapportionment is justiciable rather than a political question, and in doing so announced the six political-question factors, including textual commitment and judicially manageable standards.
- **Powell v. McCormack** (1969) [high]. Also: Mootness, Judicial review, Remedies and scope of relief
  The Court held that Powell's exclusion was a justiciable question rather than a nonjusticiable political question, reading Art. I, Section 5's textual commitment to judge 'qualifications' as limited to age, citizenship, and residency; it also held the case was not moot because the back-salary claim remained live.
- **Nixon v. United States** (1993) [high]
  The Court held that Nixon's challenge to the Senate's use of a committee to hear impeachment evidence is a nonjusticiable political question, because the Impeachment Trial Clause textually commits the 'sole Power to try' to the Senate and 'try' supplies no judicially manageable limit.
- **Rucho v. Common Cause** (2019) [high]. Also: Redistricting and gerrymandering
  The Court held that partisan gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable political questions because no judicially discoverable and manageable standards exist, and it vacated the judgments with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.
- **Goldwater Et Al. v. Carter, President of the United States, Et Al.** (1979) [medium]. Also: Ripeness, Presidential power in foreign affairs, Treaty and foreign affairs power
  The Court vacated and remanded with directions to dismiss without a majority rationale; four Justices (Rehnquist) treated the President's unilateral termination of the Taiwan treaty as a nonjusticiable political question, Powell concurred on ripeness grounds absent a congressional-executive impasse, and Brennan would have reached the merits on the President's recognition power.
- **Zivotofsky Ex Rel. Zivotofsky v. Clinton** (2012) [high]. Also: Judicial review, Presidential power in foreign affairs
  The Court held that the political question doctrine does not bar review of Zivotofsky's claim under §214(d), because deciding whether a statute is constitutional is a familiar judicial task, citing Marbury, and is not textually committed to another branch or lacking manageable standards.

### Remedies and scope of relief

- **Trump v. CASA, Inc.** (2025) [high]. Also: Congressional control of federal jurisdiction, Standing: states and government plaintiffs
  The Court held that universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority Congress granted federal courts in the Judiciary Act of 1789, limiting relief to what is necessary to give complete relief to each plaintiff with standing, without reaching the merits of the birthright-citizenship order.

## Federal Legislative Power

### Necessary and Proper Clause

- **McCulloch v. Maryland** (1819) [high]. Also: Federal supremacy and intergovernmental immunity
  The Court held that Congress could charter the Bank as an appropriate means of executing its enumerated powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause, and that Maryland's tax on the Bank was an unconstitutional state tax on a federal instrument under the Supremacy Clause.
- **M'culloch v. State of Maryland** (1819) [high]. Also: Federal supremacy and intergovernmental immunity
  The Court held that Congress could charter the Bank as an appropriate means under the Necessary and Proper Clause ('let the end be legitimate'), and that Maryland's tax on the Bank's branch was invalid because the supremacy of federal law bars states from taxing federal instrumentalities.

### Commerce Clause: foundations and pre-1937 limits

- **Gibbons v. Ogden** (1824) [high]. Also: Preemption: conflict (impossibility), Dormant Commerce Clause: foundations and rationale, Federal supremacy and intergovernmental immunity
  Marshall defined 'commerce' broadly as intercourse that includes navigation and reaches into state interiors when it concerns more than one state, then held New York's steamboat monopoly void because it collided with the federal coasting license, which is supreme under Article VI.
  > Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse.
- **Carter v. Carter Coal Co.** (1936) [high]. Also: Taxing power
  The Court held that the Act's labor provisions exceeded the commerce power because mining is local production whose effect on interstate commerce is only indirect, and it treated the 15 percent 'excise tax' as a coercive penalty that falls with those provisions rather than a valid exercise of the taxing power.
- **United States v. E. C. Knight Co.** (1895) [high]
  The Court held that manufacturing precedes and is distinct from commerce, so a sugar-refining monopoly affected interstate commerce only indirectly and could not be reached under the Sherman Act and the commerce power.
- **A. L. A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States** (1935) [high]. Also: Nondelegation
  The course note assigns Schechter for the Restrictive Era direct/indirect-effects rule, and the Court held that the defendants' slaughtering and local sales, occurring after the poultry came to rest in Brooklyn, were beyond Congress's commerce power; it also independently held NIRA § 3 an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power.
- **Hammer v. Dagenhart** (1918) [high]. Also: Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering
  The Court held 5-4 that the Keating-Owen Act's ban on interstate shipment of child-labor goods exceeded the commerce power because production is not commerce and the goods were harmless, invading powers reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment.

### Commerce Clause: modern scope

- **National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius** (2012) [high]. Also: Necessary and Proper Clause, Taxing power, Spending power and conditional grants
  The course note assigns the case for the Commerce power, and the brief reports the holding that the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses do not authorize compelling individuals to buy insurance (the activity/inactivity line), while the mandate was upheld under the taxing power and the Medicaid expansion was held coercive under the Spending Clause.
- **National Labor Relations Board v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.** (1937) [high]. Also: Commerce Clause: foundations and pre-1937 limits
  The Court upheld the NLRA as applied because intrastate labor relations at Jones & Laughlin's integrated plant had a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce, rejecting the direct/indirect and production/commerce limits of Schechter and Carter Coal.
- **United States v. Darby** (1941) [high]. Also: Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering, Necessary and Proper Clause
  Darby upheld the FLSA's ban on interstate shipment of substandard-labor goods and its wage-hour rules for production workers under the Commerce Clause, overruling Hammer v. Dagenhart, and called the Tenth Amendment a 'truism' that imposes no independent limit.
- **Wickard v. Filburn** (1942) [high]
  The unanimous Court held that Congress may regulate wheat grown for home consumption because such activity, aggregated with that of others similarly situated, substantially affects interstate commerce, rejecting the old direct/indirect and production formulas.
- **Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States** (1965) [high]. Also: State action
  The Court upheld Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 as a valid exercise of the commerce power because racial discrimination by a motel serving interstate travelers substantially burdens interstate commerce, regardless of Congress's moral motive.
- **Katzenbach v. McClung** (1964) [high]
  The Court upheld Title II as applied to Ollie's Barbecue because Congress had a rational basis to find that restaurant discrimination, aggregated under Wickard, substantially affects interstate commerce through reduced food purchases and restricted travel.
- **United States v. Lopez** (1995) [high]
  The Court held that § 922(q) exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause power because gun possession in a school zone is not economic activity substantially affecting interstate commerce, is not part of a larger economic regulatory scheme, and lacks a jurisdictional element.
- **United States v. Morrison** (2000) [high]. Also: Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power, State action
  The Court held Congress lacked Commerce Clause power to enact VAWA's civil remedy because gender-motivated violence is noneconomic and cannot be aggregated, even with extensive findings, and separately held § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action, not private individuals.
- **Gonzales v. Raich** (2005) [high]. Also: Necessary and Proper Clause
  The Court held 6-3 that the Commerce Clause, under Wickard's aggregation principle, lets Congress apply the CSA to intrastate, noncommercial medical marijuana because that activity is part of an economic class substantially affecting the interstate market, while Scalia's concurrence rested on the Necessary and Proper Clause.

### Taxing power

- **Steward MacHine Co. v. Davis** (1937) [high]. Also: Spending power and conditional grants, Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering
  The Court upheld the Social Security Act's excise tax on employers as a valid exercise of the taxing power that may reach employment as a relation of common right, and held that the 90 per cent credit for state unemployment contributions was permissible inducement, not unconstitutional coercion of the states.

### Spending power and conditional grants

- **United States v. Butler** (1936) [high]. Also: Taxing power, Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering
  Butler adopted the Hamiltonian view that Congress may tax and spend for the general welfare beyond its other enumerated powers, but held the AAA's processing tax and benefit payments invalid as a means to regulate agricultural production reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment.
- **South Dakota v. Dole** (1987) [high]. Also: Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering
  The Court upheld § 158's withholding of 5% of highway funds from states with a drinking age under 21, applying its four-part spending-power test (general welfare, unambiguous conditions, relatedness, no independent constitutional bar) plus the anti-coercion limit.
- **Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman** (1981) [high]. Also: Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power
  The Court held that § 6010 imposed no enforceable conditions on Pennsylvania because Spending Clause legislation operates like a contract, and Congress must state grant conditions unambiguously so that states can knowingly accept them; it also declined to infer that Congress acted under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Federalism and Limits on State Power

### Tenth Amendment, state autonomy, and anti-commandeering

- **Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority** (1985) [high]. Also: Commerce Clause: modern scope
  Garcia overruled National League of Cities' 'traditional governmental functions' Tenth Amendment limit and held that Congress may apply the FLSA under its commerce power to a municipal transit authority, leaving states to rely on the political process for protection.
- **New York v. United States** (1992) [high]. Also: Spending power and conditional grants, Commerce Clause: modern scope
  The Court held that Congress may encourage but not compel states to enact or administer a federal regulatory program, striking down the take-title provision as commandeering while upholding the monetary incentives (conditional spending) and access incentives (commerce power).
- **Printz v. United States** (1997) [high]. Also: Presidential power in domestic affairs, Necessary and Proper Clause
  The Court held 5-4 that the Brady Act's interim commands to chief law enforcement officers were unconstitutional because Congress may not compel state executive officers to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program, extending New York v. United States; the majority also relied on the President's control of law execution, and the dissent relied on the Necessary and Proper Clause.
- **Reno v. Condon** (2000) [high]. Also: Commerce Clause: modern scope
  The Court unanimously held that the DPPA does not commandeer the States under New York and Printz, because it regulates the States as owners and sellers of DMV databases rather than requiring them to regulate private parties. It also held the Act a valid commerce-power regulation of personal information as an article in interstate commerce.
- **Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn.** (2018) [high]. Also: Preemption: express, Remedies and scope of relief
  The Court held that PASPA's prohibition on state authorization and licensing of sports gambling violates the anticommandeering rule because Congress cannot issue direct orders to state legislatures, whether to enact or to refrain from enacting laws, and it distinguished valid preemption as regulation of private actors.
  > The PASPA provision at issue here—prohibiting state authorization of sports gambling—violates the anticom­mandeering rule.

### Preemption: express

- **Lorillard Tobacco Co. v. Reilly** (2001) [high]. Also: Commercial speech, Expressive conduct
  The Court held that the FCLAA's express pre-emption provision, read in light of the presumption against pre-emption, displaces Massachusetts' outdoor and point-of-sale cigarette advertising regulations, and then applied Central Hudson to strike down the smokeless tobacco and cigar advertising restrictions while upholding the sales-practice rules under O'Brien.
  > Accordingly, we hold that the Attorney General's outdoor and point-of-sale advertising regulations targeting cigarettes are preempted by the FCLAA.
- **Monsanto Co. v. Durnell** (2026) [high]
  The Court held that FIFRA's express preemption clause, §136v(b), bars Durnell's Missouri failure-to-warn claim because requiring a cancer warning would impose a labeling requirement 'in addition to or different from' the EPA-approved label, which counts as a requirement 'under' FIFRA; the majority expressly declined to reach implied (impossibility) preemption, which only the dissent addresses.
  > FIFRA therefore expressly preempts Durnell’s claim.
- **Riegel v. Medtronic, Inc.** (2008) [high]
  The Court held that the MDA's express preemption clause, 21 U.S.C. § 360k(a), bars state common-law tort claims challenging the design or labeling of FDA premarket-approved devices because those duties are state 'requirements'; Justice Ginsburg dissents on that reading.
  > Contextual examination of the Act convinces me that § 360k(a)’s inclusion of the term “requirement” should not prompt a sweeping preemption of mine-run claims for relief under state tort law.
- **Chamber of Commerce of United States of America v. Whiting** (2011) [high]. Also: Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes)
  The Court held that Arizona's law suspending or revoking business licenses of employers who knowingly hire unauthorized aliens falls within IRCA's saving clause for "licensing and similar laws" and so is not expressly preempted; it also rejected obstacle-preemption challenges to both the licensing sanctions and the E-Verify mandate.
  > We hold that Arizona’s licensing law falls well within the confines of the authority Congress chose to leave to the States and therefore is not expressly preempted.

### Preemption: conflict (impossibility)

- **Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul** (1963) [high]. Also: Preemption: field, Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes), Dormant Commerce Clause: facially neutral laws with discriminatory purpose or effect
  The Court held California's 8% oil-content avocado standard was not preempted by federal marketing orders because dual compliance was not physically impossible (growers could leave fruit on trees longer) and Congress showed no clear intent to occupy the field, while remanding the Commerce Clause burden/discrimination claim for a new trial.
  > A holding of federal exclusion of state law is inescapable and requires no inquiry into congressional design where compliance with both federal and state regulations is a *143 physical impossibility for one engaged in interstate commerce, cf. Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U. S. 364, 399-401 ; Morgan v. Virginia, 328 U. S. 373 ; Bibb v. Navajo Freight Lines, Inc., 359 U. S. 520 .
- **PLIVA, Inc. v. Mensing** (2011) [high]
  The Court held that state failure-to-warn claims against generic drug makers are preempted under impossibility preemption because federal law required generic labels to match the brand-name label, so the manufacturers could not independently do what state law required.
  > Thus, it was impossible for the Manufacturers to comply with both their state-law duty to change the label and their federal-law duty to keep the label the same.
- **Mut. Pharm. Co. v. Bartlett** (2013) [high]. Also: Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes)
  The Court held New Hampshire's warning-based design-defect claim impliedly pre-empted because it was impossible for Mutual to satisfy a state-law duty to strengthen sulindac's label while obeying the federal bar on unilateral generic label changes, and it rejected the 'stop-selling' rationale as incompatible with impossibility pre-emption.
  > In the instant case, it was impossible for Mutual to comply with both its state-law duty to strengthen the warnings on sulindac's label and its federal-law duty not to alter sulindac's label.

### Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes)

- **Arizona v. United States** (2012) [medium]. Also: Preemption: field, Ripeness
  The Court held §§ 5(C) and 6 of S.B. 1070 preempted as obstacles to the purposes of federal immigration law and § 3 preempted because Congress occupied the field of alien registration, while declining to enjoin § 2(B) before state courts construed it.
  > It follows that a state law to the contrary is an obstacle to the regulatory system Congress chose.
- **Wyeth v. Levine** (2009) [high]. Also: Preemption: conflict (impossibility)
  The course note assigns Wyeth as an impediment (obstacle) preemption case, and the Court held that Levine's failure-to-warn claim did not obstruct congressional purposes because the FDCA's history shows Congress did not intend to preempt state tort suits; it also rejected impossibility preemption because the CBE regulation let Wyeth strengthen its warning unilaterally.
  > Its silence on the issue, coupled with its certain awareness of the prevalence of state tort litigation, is powerful evidence that Congress did not intend FDA oversight to be the exclusive means of ensuring drug safety and effectiveness.

### Preemption: field

- **Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. State Energy Resources Conservation & Development Commission** (1983) [medium]. Also: Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes), Preemption: conflict (impossibility), Ripeness
  The Court held that the Atomic Energy Act occupies the field of nuclear safety but leaves states their traditional economic authority, so California's moratorium, accepted as economically motivated, falls outside the occupied field; it also rejected the impossibility-conflict and obstacle arguments and found the § 25524.2 challenge ripe but the § 25524.1(b) challenge unripe.
  > Accordingly, the statute lies outside the occupied field of nuclear safety regulation.
- **Hines v. Davidowitz** (1941) [medium]. Also: Preemption: obstacle (impediment to federal purposes), Treaty and foreign affairs power
  The Court held Pennsylvania's alien registration law unenforceable because Congress, acting in the foreign-affairs-sensitive area of immigration, created a single integrated and all-embracing federal registration system. That is field preemption as the course note frames it, though the opinion also states the famous 'obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress' test.
  > Having the constitutional authority so to do, it has provided a standard for alien registration in a single integrated and all-embracing system in order to obtain the information deemed to be desirable in connection with aliens.

### Dormant Commerce Clause: foundations and rationale

- **Cooley v. Board of Wardens of Port of Philadelphia Ex Rel. Soc. for Relief of Distressed Pilots** (1852) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: facially discriminatory laws, Commerce Clause: foundations and pre-1937 limits
  The course note files Cooley under facially discriminatory state laws, but the holding adopts 'selective exclusiveness': the commerce grant bars state regulation only of subjects national in nature, so Pennsylvania may regulate local pilotage absent conflicting federal law. That is a foundational dormant Commerce Clause ruling, not a discrimination analysis.
  > Whatever subjects of this power are in their nature national, or admit only of one uniform system, or plan of regulation, may justly be said to be of such a nature as to require exclusive legislation by Congress.

### Dormant Commerce Clause: facially discriminatory laws

- **Tennessee Wine and Spirits Retailers Assn. v. Thomas** (2019) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: foundations and rationale
  The Court held that Tennessee's 2-year durational-residency requirement for liquor retailers facially discriminates against nonresidents, fails the narrow-tailoring test because nondiscriminatory alternatives exist, and is not saved by Section 2 of the Twenty-first Amendment, after reaffirming at length the historical rationale for the dormant Commerce Clause.
  > Since the 2-year residency requirement discriminates on its face against nonresidents, it could not be sustained if it applied across the board to all those seeking to operate any retail business in the State.
- **City of Philadelphia v. New Jersey** (1978) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: foundations and rationale
  The Court struck down New Jersey's ban on out-of-state waste as a facially discriminatory, protectionist barrier subject to a virtually per se rule of invalidity, rejecting the quarantine-law defense because no reason apart from origin justified treating out-of-state waste differently.
  > Both on its face and in its plain effect, ch. 363 violates this principle of nondiscrimination.

### Dormant Commerce Clause: facially neutral laws with discriminatory purpose or effect

- **West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy** (1994) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: facially discriminatory laws
  The Court struck down Massachusetts' milk pricing order because, although its assessment applied evenhandedly to all milk, coupling it with a subsidy paid only to in-state farmers gave it the purpose and effect of a protective tariff; the course note files the case under facially discriminatory laws, but the holding concerns a facially neutral scheme judged by its practical operation.
  > Its avowed purpose and its undisputed effect are to enable higher cost Massachusetts dairy farmers to compete with lower cost dairy farmers in other States.
- **Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Commission** (1977) [high]. Also: Standing: associational and organizational standing
  The Court held that North Carolina's facially neutral ban on state apple grades discriminated against Washington apples in practical effect, shifting to the State a burden it failed to meet on local benefits and nondiscriminatory alternatives; it also announced the three-part test for associational standing.
  > As the District Court correctly found, the challenged statute has the practical effect of not only burdening interstate sales of Washington apples, but also discriminating against them.
- **Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland** (1978) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: nondiscriminatory burdens (Pike balancing), Economic substantive due process, Preemption: conflict (impossibility)
  The Court held that Maryland's facially neutral ban on refiner-operated gas stations did not discriminate against interstate commerce in effect, even though its burden fell entirely on out-of-state firms, because the Commerce Clause protects the interstate market rather than particular interstate firms; it also rejected the undue-burden, substantive due process, and Robinson-Patman preemption claims.
  > The fact that the burden of a state regulation falls on some interstate companies does not, by itself, establish a claim of discrimination against interstate commerce.
- **Minnesota v. Clover Leaf Creamery Co.** (1981) [medium]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: nondiscriminatory burdens (Pike balancing), Rational basis review
  The Court rejected the claim that Minnesota's plastic-milk-container ban was protectionist in purpose or effect, finding it evenhanded and so applying Pike balancing (burden not clearly excessive); it also upheld the classification under deferential rational-basis equal protection review.
  > A nondiscriminatory regulation serving substantial state purposes is not invalid simply because it causes some business to shift from a predominantly out-of-state industry to a predominantly in-state industry.
- **C & a Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown** (1994) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: nondiscriminatory burdens (Pike balancing), Dormant Commerce Clause: laws favoring state or local government
  The majority held that Clarkstown's facially neutral flow control ordinance, by requiring all solid waste to be processed at a single designated local transfer station, discriminates against interstate commerce in practical effect, like earlier local processing requirements, and is invalid absent a showing that no nondiscriminatory alternative exists. O'Connor's concurrence would instead strike it down under Pike balancing, and Souter's dissent argues that favoring a municipal facility is not protectionist.
  > Though the Clarkstown ordinance may not in explicit terms seek to regulate interstate commerce, it does so nonetheless by its practical effect and design.

### Dormant Commerce Clause: laws favoring state or local government

- **United Haulers Ass'n v. Oneida-Herkimer Solid Waste Management Authority** (2007) [high]. Also: Dormant Commerce Clause: facially neutral laws with discriminatory purpose or effect, Dormant Commerce Clause: nondiscriminatory burdens (Pike balancing)
  The Court held that flow control ordinances favoring a publicly owned waste facility, while treating all private businesses alike, do not discriminate against interstate commerce; a plurality then upheld them under Pike balancing. The course note files the case under facially neutral laws with discriminatory purpose or effect, but the holding turns on favoritism toward local government.
  > Because the question is now squarely presented on the facts of the case before us, we decide that such flow control ordinances do not discriminate against interstate commerce for purposes of the dormant Commerce Clause.

## The Reconstruction Amendments and State Action

### State action

- **United States v. Guest** (1966) [medium]. Also: Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power, Vagueness and overbreadth
  The course note assigns Guest for Section 5 power over private parties, but the majority held that equal-protection rights under § 241 require state involvement and found the alleged false arrests 'with official connivance' sufficient state action; the six-Justice § 5 view appears only in the separate opinions (later treated as dicta in Morrison), so state action is primary, with Section 5 enforcement and the vagueness ruling as secondary.

### Thirteenth Amendment and its enforcement power

- **Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.** (1968) [high]. Also: State action, Race classifications
  The Court held that § 1982 bars purely private racial discrimination in property sales and is a valid exercise of Congress's Thirteenth Amendment § 2 power to rationally determine and eliminate the badges and incidents of slavery.
- **Runyon v. McCrary** (1976) [high]. Also: Expressive association, Family, marriage, and parental rights
  The Court held that 42 U.S.C. § 1981 reaches private schools' racially motivated refusals to contract, following Jones. It upheld the statute as a valid exercise of Congress's Thirteenth Amendment power, and rejected the schools' free-association, parental-rights, and privacy defenses.

### Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power

- **Civil Rights Cases** (1883) [high]. Also: State action, Thirteenth Amendment and its enforcement power
  The course note assigns the case in the module on the power to enforce the Reconstruction Amendments. The Court held that the 1875 Act's public-accommodations sections exceeded Congress's power under both amendments: Section 5 permits only corrective legislation against state action, and Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment does not reach private discrimination as a badge of slavery.
- **Katzenbach v. Morgan** (1966) [high]. Also: Voting rights, Necessary and Proper Clause
  The Court held that § 4(e) of the Voting Rights Act was a proper exercise of Congress's § 5 power to enforce the Fourteenth Amendment under a McCulloch 'plainly adapted' standard, even without a judicial finding that New York's English literacy requirement itself violated equal protection.
- **City of Boerne v. Flores** (1997) [high]. Also: Free Exercise Clause, Judicial review
  The Court held RFRA exceeded Congress's Section 5 enforcement power as applied to the states because Section 5 is remedial and requires 'congruence and proportionality' between the injury and the means, which RFRA's universal, open-ended compelling-interest mandate lacked.
- **Louisiana v. Callais** (2026) [medium]. Also: Redistricting and gerrymandering, Voting rights, Race classifications
  The Court construed VRA §2 to impose liability only on a strong inference of intentional discrimination so as to keep it within Congress's Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power, and on that reading held that §2 did not require District 6, so SB8 failed strict scrutiny as a racial gerrymander.
- **Shelby County v. Holder** (2013) [high]. Also: Voting rights
  The Court held that Section 4(b)'s coverage formula exceeded Congress's power to enforce the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments because its 'current burdens' were not justified by 'current needs' and it departed from equal sovereignty among the States without a basis in current conditions; the case is assigned in the course module on enforcing those Amendments against the states.

## Freedom of Speech and the Press

### Compelled speech

- **303 Creative LLC v. Elenis** (2023) [medium]. Also: Ripeness
  The course note assigns the case for ripeness and the credible-threat test for pre-enforcement review, but the brief shows the Court's holding turns entirely on the Free Speech Clause: Colorado may not use CADA to compel a website designer to create expressive designs conveying messages she disagrees with. Following the holding, compelled speech is primary, and ripeness is kept as secondary for students filtering by the note's topic.
